# Register: pointing at the viewer, and how hard to push

Two craft rules that decide whether a script lands. The hook mechanisms in SKILL.md assume both.

## The script points at the viewer, never at you

This is the failure that survives every other check, because a line can be
truthful, inside its word budget, and still be about the wrong person.

The general rule is to write to the reader,
never about the writer": count "I", "we", "my", "our" against "you", "your", and
if the first group wins, rewrite. What follows is the video-specific form, where
the same failure costs a swipe inside three seconds.

**Banned hook shapes**, all of them autobiography:

- The dated artefact: "I made this in 2023", "I published this two years ago".
- The origin story: "When I started", "I used to", "A few years ago I".
- Any credential the audience already knows you have.
- Any line that contextualizes before it threatens: background, "so here's the
  thing", or an announcement of what the video will cover.
- Any line that merely informs. "Here are six ClickUp terms" is a table of
  contents. A viewer does not stay for a table of contents.

**The test.** Could a stranger who has never heard of you hear this line and
immediately feel that something they own is broken? If the answer is no, the hook
is about you and it has to be rewritten. Your history is at best a beat in the
middle, and usually it is a cut.

**The same test runs on the close.** The last line is the second-most-heard line
in the piece, and it is where the author creeps back in: "that's what I built",
"I said this three times". Reach for the second-person version of the same beat
and the line gets stronger, because it hands the viewer something to do. First
person survives in the middle only where it is carrying evidence: a number you
measured, a mistake you made, a thing you actually did that the viewer can check.

**Then make every beat in the body earn its place.** The hook is not allowed to be
the only place doing work. A bare definition is a glossary entry, and a glossary
entry is where people swipe. What rescues a beat depends on the piece:

| Piece | What each beat needs | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Persuasion**: a claim, a diagnosis, a contrarian take | A consequence loaded onto the fact | "Folder. Holds Lists, never tasks. This is where most workspaces go flat." |
| **Teaching**: a cheat sheet, a walkthrough, a reference | A concrete worked example, or a recommendation the viewer can act on | "Maybe you want to record the client budget in US dollars. By default ClickUp doesn't do that, but you can add a custom field." |

**Do not put threats in a teaching piece.** Somebody who came to learn the words
did not come to be frightened about their workspace, and stacking a consequence
onto every definition reads as pressure with no release. Prescription and a real
example carry a teaching beat further than fear does. Close a persuasion piece on
a command, and a teaching piece on the offer of the artefact.

For the register itself, and for what the speaker actually sounds like out loud, the
source of truth is your own voice profile, and inside it
`references/measured.md`, section "Spoken voice". Do not restate it here.

## The emotional register

Pick the lever first, then find the true material that carries it. Full taxonomy in `references/emotional-angles-upstream.md`.

| Lever | The move | Needs to be true |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shame-release** | Take the blame off the viewer and put it on the tool or the system | The system really is the problem |
| **Fear** | Name the thing that is already broken and load the consequence onto it: "Your X is missing Y, and everything you built sits on top of it" | The thing really is broken, and the consequence really follows |
| **Anxiety** | Hand them a check they can run right now and might fail: "Open it. If you see Y, you built it wrong" | The check is real and can actually come back positive |
| **FOMO** | Something shipped, other people have it, they do not | It shipped, it is dated, and they plausibly do not have it |
| **Loss aversion** | Frame what they are already losing, not what they could gain | The loss is measured, ideally on your own numbers first |
| **Identity split** | Name the two kinds of people and let them pick a side | The split is real and the viewer can tell which side they are on |
| **Revenge arc** | You were failed by something, you stopped tolerating it, here is what you did | You actually did the thing |
| **Tribal callout** | Address one group so precisely that everyone else scrolls | You know that group well enough to be right about them |
| **Curiosity gap** | Show the shape of what they do not know | The answer arrives. A withheld answer is bait and gets punished |

**Shame-release is usually the strongest of these and the most underused.** It converts a viewer who feels bad about themselves into a viewer who feels bad about a product, and it costs nothing in honesty when the product really is the problem.

**Cialdini sits underneath the lever, and is never named on camera.** Use it to
choose the shape, then delete the vocabulary:

- **Commitment and consistency**: make them perform a small act, "open it", "count
  them". A viewer who acts is a viewer who stays.
- **Scarcity**: a level, a setting or a window they do not have yet.
- **Social proof**: what happens in every workspace you open, stated only where the
  claim survives the truth rule above.
- **Authority**: earned through the specificity of the diagnosis, never claimed.
- **Loss aversion**: what they are losing now beats what they could gain later.
