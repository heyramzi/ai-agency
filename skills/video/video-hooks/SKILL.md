# Video Hooks

The opening decides whether people **stay**. It does not decide how many arrive.

That distinction was measured. Across 27 transcripts from Matt Gray's channel, the openings of
his hits and his flops are the same length (151 words against 156), reach the
viewer at the same moment, and reach their first number at the same moment. The
cleanest case is a pair of videos that open with the identical sentence, "at the
time of this video, I'm 35 years old", and sit eleven times apart in views. The
finding replicated on a second channel in an opposite niche.

**The subject decides reach, then the title and thumbnail, and only then is the
hook ever heard.** If a video underperformed on views, do not rewrite the hook.
Check the subject was worth making, rewrite the title, and regenerate the
thumbnail (`youtube-thumbnail`). Band your own niche before trusting any title lever:
the method is in that skill's `references/banding.md`.

Where this skill earns its keep is everything after the click: the swipe-away
window on a Short, and whether a long-form viewer is still there at ninety
seconds. That is retention, it is what completion rate measures, and on Shorts
completion rate is what triggers promotion. So the hook still matters enormously.
It just cannot be held responsible for a number it never touched.

**A dip straight after an off-target spike is a cohort problem, not a hook problem.**
When a video reaches far outside the ICP, the next few get served to that borrowed
audience and land flat. It reads as a content failure and it is a distribution one, so
do not rewrite a format that was working. Judge the spike itself on joins rather than
views. Keep a count of views to email or community joins for exactly this reason: a
thousand views from your buyer is worth more than a hundred thousand from nobody in
particular. This one is a hypothesis, not control-tested. Test it on your own channel
before acting on it.

**This skill stops at 0:30.** Everything after the open, the teach blocks, the demo
choreography, the runtime budget and where the one commercial ask goes, is
`video-script`. It carries the three-run control that settles ask placement, and it
takes its opening from here rather than writing its own.

## The one rule that overrides the rest

**Go as hard as you want on the mechanism. Every fact underneath it has to be true.**

These are two separate dials and they get confused constantly. The levers in the table below are neutral: use any of them. What is not available is inventing the material they operate on: a number nobody measured, a story that did not happen, a stake that does not exist.

This is a positioning constraint before it is anything else. If you sell on being the operator who shows the real numbers, one fabricated claim is the only thing that can cost you that. Keep a proof table, one row per claim with the artefact that measured it, and pull from it rather than writing from memory.

The craft argument points the same way. Invented drama lands generic, because a model reaching for stakes reaches for the average stakes. A real number is specific, and specific is what stops a thumb. **"You lose about 24 hours a month" is weaker than "I lost 23 hours and 48 minutes this month, here is the screen."**

"Manufactured urgency feels fake" is a note about invented material, not a ban on emotion. It is written against fiction, not against feeling.

**Show the number, do not only say it.** Where a real artefact exists (a dashboard, a counter, a board, an invoice), the proof frame is that artefact, within two seconds of the claim.

**The strongest proof frame is two artefacts from one input.** One prompt, two configurations, both
outputs on screen, then a verdict that says which half each one won. A single screenshot asks the
viewer to trust the comparison; a pair hands them the control and lets them see it. The verdict is
the payload, so it has to split rather than crown: "this one won the format, that one won the
structure, so keep the first for taste and loosen the second for procedure" carries a rule the
viewer can apply, where "the second one is better" carries a preference.

It costs one real experiment and it is the cheapest authority available on a topic where everyone
else is asserting. The truth rule binds it hardest: both artefacts have to exist, come from the same
input, and be shown rather than described. One run is one run, so say so on camera.

Single source, no control test: Nate Herk, "I Deleted All My Claude Skills... And Claude Got
Smarter", 12 Aug 2026, 71k views in 5 days. 716 seconds, 2,902 words (243 wpm), 4 clips borrowed
from a 35-minute Y Combinator interview, each followed by operator translation. The A/B on two
resource guides is the only original evidence in the piece and it is what the video is remembered
for.

## Banned constructions

Keep a banned word and phrase list, enforced rather than advisory, in exactly one
place, and add to it there rather than to a local copy. A lint rule over your own
scripts is what makes it enforced.

**The negation pivot is the one this skill gets wrong most often.** Defining a
thing by denying another thing first: "It is not a project, it is a wish." "Not
the price. The thing." "I did not get more creative, I stopped scheduling myself
out of it." A single one is a rhetorical beat. Several in a ninety-second script
is the loudest signal in the piece that a machine wrote it.

**One per script, maximum**, and only when it is short and concrete. Cut every
abstract pivot where both halves are concepts. Count them before shipping; the
pattern is invisible while drafting and obvious on playback. The tailing variant
("...not X" appended to a finished clause) counts too.

## Point at the viewer, and pick the register

A script addresses the viewer's situation rather than reporting yours, and the emotional register is chosen rather than defaulted. See [`references/register.md`](references/register.md).

**A hook is ultra specific or it is not a hook**, always, because people need to
identify. The viewer has to be able to run the claim against
their own week and come back with yes or no. "Your business does not run without you" is a general
truth, so nobody answers it; "if you run an agency and it stops the week you go on holiday, you are
the system" names a week the viewer either had or did not. The shape that forces this is a
conditional: `if you <a situation with a number, a tool or a person in it>`, then the verdict.

The identifier is the audience filter as well. A hook that names the wrong people loses nobody worth
keeping, and a hook that names 28-year-olds in Minnesota working at Walmart keeps exactly them.
Losing the rest of the feed is the point of the line, not a cost of it.

**Then stop, because the hook must not answer itself.** A hook that names the situation
and delivers the verdict in the same breath has nothing left to create intrigue with.
Two mechanisms are running and the second one dies on contact with a payoff.
Self-reference gets the sentence processed as being about the viewer (Kelley et al. 2002; the
recall effect goes back to Rogers 1977), and the missing main clause leaves it unfinished, which is
what keeps it live (Zeigarnik 1927) and what Loewenstein's information gap, 1994, calls the felt
distance between what you know and what you want to know. The gap has to be small, specific and
visible, so a general claim opens none. Say "if your account manager spends 2 hours a day updating
a sheet" and let the next line, the next slide or the next second carry the verdict.

Numbers in a hook are digits, never words, on screen and in the caption alike. "3 hours"
stops a thumb; "three hours" reads as prose and does not.

## Two windows, two jobs

| Format | Window | Budget | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shorts, Reels, TikTok** | first **1 to 3 seconds** | 8 to 15 words | Stop the swipe. One claim, no setup. |
| **Long-form** | first **30 seconds** | 65 to 85 words | Earn the next minute. Claim, then credibility, then promise. |

**The word budgets above are a slower register than the category runs, and that is deliberate.**
Measured 11 Aug 2026 across 20 long-form videos from three AI-agency channels, 13.5 hours:
**204 to 262 words per minute, mean 232.** At that pace a 30-second open is about 115 words, not
65 to 85. Holding the tighter budget means speaking slower and saying fewer things, which
is the register; it is not an accident to be corrected upward. Re-measure this on your own
category before quoting it: transcribe twenty videos and divide words by runtime.

Three more things that pass replicated across those 20 and are worth knowing before writing:

- **18 of 20 open on a claim and none of those says hello first.** The two that open chatty are
  the same creator's, and both sit at the bottom of his view counts in the set. Independent
  support for the banned hook shapes above.
- **The close is one formula in 19 of 20**: recap, free resource, paid step, next-video handoff,
  signoff, which stacks two or three asks. One creator runs the same closing sentence in six of
  seven videos. The single-ask close this skill already requires is the differentiator, not a
  constraint to apologise for.
- **The first call to action does not wait for the end.** One channel's median lands at 12% of
  runtime and its three-hour flagship plugs at 1%. Holding every ask to the last 30 seconds is a
  choice, not the norm.

**A counted promise is a contract.** An open that says "three things everyone has to be doing" owes
the viewer three beats they can count, each one named on screen. Breaking it is the most common
failure in the borrowed-clip format: the number buys the click and the body then wanders through
commentary, so the viewer leaves holding one idea and a sense of having been managed. Either
enumerate on delivery, or make the promise without the number.

**Refusing the category's own promise is the cheapest credibility available**, and it is missing
from the lever table because it is not an emotional lever, it is a trust move. In the strongest
opening measured, the creator states inside 30 seconds that he will not promise a revenue figure
in a week, and that the people who do are setting you up to fail. It costs one sentence, it needs
no number, and it only works in a niche where everyone else is making that promise. Check that
they are before spending the sentence.

For the short window: `references/shorts-playbook.md` for the algorithm and specs. The metric that matters is viewed versus swiped away. **Above 75% is good, below 50% means the hook is broken**, not the video.

For the long window: the four jobs below, then `SKILL.md`'s execution steps. The vendored
long-form hook writer and its retention guide were removed on 29 Aug 2026 with the rest of the
vendor files; `mechanisms.md` and `gap-shapes.md` carry what this skill actually uses.

### The long open has a fifth job the Short does not: make the viewer feel safe

**BRENS: big, relatable, easy, new, safe.** Score a long-form open against the five and take the
most you can get honestly. Four of them restate levers already in the table above (big is scale,
relatable is the tribal callout, new is FOMO, easy is the named method). **Safe is the one this
skill was missing**, and it only applies past about thirty minutes: the viewer is deciding whether
to spend an hour, and the open has to tell them the hour will not be wasted. It is answered by
naming who is talking and what they will walk away with, in one sentence, and it costs nothing in
honesty.

Safe is also why the promise has to be about the **topic rather than the tool**. Say inside the
open that the method holds if they use the other tool, and the viewer stops calculating whether
their stack disqualifies them. This is the same trust move as refusing the category's promise
above: one sentence, no number, and it widens the audience the piece can pay off for.

Single source, no control test. It sits below anything measured on your own channel.

### The hook outline: borrow the shape, not the subject

When an opening on another channel clearly worked, condense it to a **hook outline**, meaning the
intro written out beat by beat plus a one-line-per-section outline of the rest. Then pour your own
substance into that skeleton. The structure is one brick, and holding it constant while changing the topic is the highest-hit-rate
remix available. Two constraints carry over unchanged, and they are the ones that get dropped: every
fact underneath the borrowed shape has to be yours and measured, and the winner still goes through
a voice pass against a real speech sample of your own. A borrowed structure in a borrowed
voice is the failure this produces.

## The whole Short, not just the hook

When the job is a full Short script rather than an opening line, read
`references/shorts-structures.md` before drafting. It holds the skeleton
(outcome hook → named method → stepped body with a concrete carrier per step →
mid-flip rehook at ~25 seconds → recap naming the parts → reply-trigger CTA
with a named artefact), the retention structures behind it (Modern Story Arc,
Dopamine Addiction Loop, Anticipation Loading, Mid Flip Rehook), an annotated
worked example, and a fill-in template. The material is distilled from one
short-form corpus and is marked hypothesis, not control-tested; the truth rule
and the voice pass still apply to every line it produces.

## The five mechanisms

Shock or contradiction, problem agitation, story open, curiosity gap, social proof. Name the
one before writing, because an unnamed hook usually turns out to be two fighting. How each
works, what it suits and how it fails: [references/mechanisms.md](references/mechanisms.md).

## Execution

0. **The open is written out in full, and the body it feeds is written as bullets.** The
   hook's opening lines are the one place in a script where the exact wording is
   load-bearing, so they ship verbatim and marked `Say this line as written`. Everything
   after them is bullets: `video-script`, "Every script ships as bullets".
1. **On long-form, write the open last, from the finished transcript.** Outline, record the body,
   then read what was actually said and write the open against that. A long recording goes where it
   goes, and an open written in advance ends up promising the video that was planned rather than the
   one that exists, which is a retention loss at the exact moment it costs most. This does not apply
   to a Short, where the hook is the plan.
2. **Find the true number first.** Before writing a word, ask what is already measured and screenshottable. If nothing is, say so and stop; that is the real blocker.
3. **Pick the window**, 1 to 3 seconds or 30 seconds, and hold the word budget. Going over is the most common failure.
4. **Write at least three variants on different mechanisms.** One variant is not a choice.
5. **Rate each one**: mechanism, word count, drop-off risk with a reason, and what makes it fail.
6. **Read them aloud.** A hook that cannot be said in one breath is not a hook.
7. **Run the voice pass.** Read the winner against a real speech sample of the person who will say it. A mechanically correct hook in the wrong voice still loses.
8. If this run surfaced a failure mode not already listed below, append it to Learned Patterns.

## Verification checklist

- [ ] The first line describes the viewer's problem, not the author's history, and survives the stranger test
- [ ] The first-person count was taken across the whole script and it is zero, or every survivor is a hedge on the speaker's own number, the DM ask, an assignment, or a line somebody else said. The hook itself carries none: an opening about the writer spends the only attention the video is guaranteed
- [ ] The closing line points at the viewer's next action, not at what the author did
- [ ] Every middle beat carries a consequence rather than a definition, and the close is a command rather than a recap
- [ ] An emotional lever was chosen by name before the hook was written, and the hook is not merely informative
- [ ] Every number in the hook is measured, not estimated, and is traceable to a source or a screen
- [ ] The first frame shows the proof where a real artefact exists, and any A/B claim puts both artefacts on screen from the same input
- [ ] Any counted promise in the open is paid by that many named, countable beats in the body
- [ ] Word count is inside the budget for the chosen window
- [ ] On long-form, the open was written against the recorded transcript rather than the plan, and past thirty minutes it answers the safe beat
- [ ] Three or more variants were written on different named mechanisms
- [ ] Each variant carries a drop-off risk rating with a reason
- [ ] The winner survived being read aloud in one breath
- [ ] The winner was read against a real speech sample of the person who will say it
- [ ] Negation pivots were counted: at most one in the script, short and concrete
- [ ] Any new failure mode was appended to Learned Patterns

## Closing a run

This skill appends new failure modes to its own pattern list after each run. If this run
surfaced one that is not already listed, append it to Learned Patterns before finishing.
Any run that writes or fixes a script or an opening loads this skill first, and anything
measured about how a category opens or closes gets appended here before the turn ends.

## Learned Patterns

Appended when a run surfaces something this skill did not already know. Newest first.

- Rating a hook for craft says nothing about whether it is aimed at the right person. A
  line can be tight, proof-carrying and rejected on sight because it is autobiography and
  the viewer has no stake in it. Check whose problem the first line describes before
  rating anything else.
- An anxiety hook is only as good as the odds the viewer actually fails the test. One
  draft told the viewer to open a tool and check for a state that the tool cannot produce.
  Verify the failure state exists before shipping the line.
- The correction to a flat hook is the hook, not the whole register. Told to make a weak
  opening emotional, a run swung a nine-beat teaching piece to fear on every beat and the
  calm version with worked examples was better. Ask which of the two the piece is before
  choosing what every beat carries.
- A term list with a strong hook and a flat body is still a flat body. Load a consequence
  onto every beat and close on a command rather than a recap.
- Count the words before judging whether an opening is good. The Shorts window is 1 to 3
  seconds, which is 8 to 15 words; a 25-word sentence is already broken however it reads.
- Ask what the product already reports about itself before writing anything. One hook was
  drafted around "I built this myself" while a screen showing 23h 48m saved and 71,404
  words dictated sat unused in the same project.
- A credential only works as social proof when the viewer does not already have it. On a
  personal-brand account the audience knows your background, so it buys nothing.
- When a piece is organised around a downloadable, a reply-trigger CTA ("reply with the
  word and I will send it") beats pointing at a community, and the artefact must exist
  before publish.
- Never build a fresh rules file in a scratch directory to brief subagents. The scattered
  copy went stale inside the same session. Briefs point at this file; new craft rules are
  edited into it and nowhere else.
