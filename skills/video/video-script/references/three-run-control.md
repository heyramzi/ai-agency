# The three-run control

Tom Youngs re-ran one title construction three times: same channel, same offer,
same shape, one variable changed per run. This is the cleanest natural experiment
in the corpus, because everything that usually confounds a comparison is held
still.

| | Nov 2024 | May 2025 | Nov 2025 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Views | **89,117** | 52,513 | 17,873 |
| Runtime | **19:19** | 29:54 | 28:27 |
| Words | **3,843** | 5,573 | 5,496 |
| Pace | 199 wpm | 186 wpm | 193 wpm |
| Promise in the title | $20,000 | $20,000 | **$100,000** |
| First ask that leaves the video | 98% of runtime | 99% | **11%** |
| Arithmetic on screen | none | one aside | **90 seconds** |
| His own failure, told | **in full** | one line | one line |

The older videos have had longer to accumulate. Adjusted to equal age the $20,000
runs sit around 34,000 to 40,000, so the smaller promise still wins by roughly 2x
rather than 5x. Say the adjustment out loud whenever this table is cited.

Five rules come straight off it.

1. **Nineteen minutes beat twenty-nine, and the corpus says the danger is the
   other end.** The evidence is in words, 3,600 to 4,200 at 190 to 200 wpm, but the
   script is budgeted in beats, because a word budget is a budget on writing and the
   only way to spend one is to write sentences. See the beat budget below. A script
   past 4,500 words is not more thorough, it is the losing run.
   **Measured 26 Aug 2026 on 3,556 long-form videos across 13 channels**, era-adjusted
   so channel growth cannot pose as technique: 13 to 18 minutes runs 1.09x its own
   channel and holds on 8 channels of 12, 18 to 25 runs 1.07x on 9 of 13, and 25 to 35
   still runs 1.04x on 8 of 12. **Under 13 minutes is where the corpus punishes: 0.88x,
   beating its own channel on 3 channels in 12.** So the band this rule already targets
   is the right one, and the failure it should be read against is a thin script rather
   than a long one. The three-run control is what says 29 minutes of the *same* material
   loses; it is not evidence that 25 minutes of more material does.
   **This governs guide and offer videos, which is every video the channel makes.**
   A packaged course is a different format and the control does not reach it: there
   completeness is the promise, chapters are the navigation, and length reads as
   proof rather than as cost. Nick Saraev's four-hour Claude Code course took
   2,314,591 views on 502k subscribers, a 4.6x channel multiplier, 23 Aug 2026.
   Named here so it is not mistaken for a licence: **what transfers from a course is
   register, never runtime.** Nothing on this channel is packaged as a course yet, so
   until one is, the band above is the band.
2. **The one commercial ask sits in the last twenty seconds.** See the ask rule
   below, which is where this collides with the generic advice and wins.
3. **Tell your own failure in full, in the viewer's words.** The winner is the only
   run that tells the origin story properly: the agency that nearly broke him, the
   margins, the crying on Zoom. The two losers give it one line. Our buyer's life is
   the story, so a script that skips it is a manual. **The failure is the one thing
   about the writer that is allowed on camera, and only because the viewer is living
   it.** The test is whether they can see themselves inside the sentence. A
   credential, a process or a good week cannot pass it, whatever tense it is in, and
   everywhere else in the script every "I" is rewritten as a "you" or cut. Stated
   20 Aug 2026, cutting four Shorts to a quarter of the raw take: "People don't care
   about me. Every I should be a you." `humanizer`, "On camera, every I is a you".
   **When there is no failure of his on the topic, the block is the viewer's week in the
   second person, and nothing is invented to fill it.** Found 24 Aug 2026 writing EC51: the
   subject was client follow-up and the corpus holds no failure of the speaker's about it, so the
   block became the first client the viewer ever landed and the six months of silence after
   the project shipped. That is still this block doing its job, because what it is for is
   the buyer recognising their own life, and `voice-dna`'s tone target is already written in
   exactly that grammar: "Three weeks later, nothing is where you expect it to be."
   The failure of the block is a borrowed one. The reference this rule is derived from is
   Tom Youngs telling his own story, and putting *his* margins and *his* Zoom call in
   the speaker's mouth would be the invented material `video-hooks` bans, not an application of
   this rule.
   **The second permitted "I" is two words long and it joins the viewer's mistake.** "Everyone was
   building their second brain in Notion. Me included." The speaker puts himself inside the crowd
   being criticised, which is the opposite of the "I" the rule bans: it costs the viewer nothing
   and it stops the critique reading as a man standing above the thing he is describing. Anything
   longer is a story about the author again.
4. **Never compute on screen.** Ninety seconds of arithmetic appears only in the
   losing run. Show the number and its artefact; do not derive it. `video-hooks`
   already holds the proof-frame rule this depends on.
5. **The promise number is one the viewer can picture reaching next quarter.**
   $100,000 addresses the arrived, $20,000 addresses the aspirant, and the
   aspirant outnumbers them. Matt Gray found the same thing independently when
   one script addressed to "your 20s or 30s" beat "your 30s or 40s" by 11x.

## What corroborates the ask rule, and what cannot

Moved out of `SKILL.md` on 29 Aug 2026 so the rule carries the page and the evidence carries this
one. The rule itself is unchanged: exactly one outbound ask, in the last twenty seconds.

**Corroborated at scale, on a channel that owes us nothing.** Saraev's four-hour course carries no
subscribe ask, no like ask, four mentions of files "down below", one product name that appears
inside a demo, and a 20-second pitch at 04:10:11 that he closes with "that's my last and only pitch
of this video". Same split, 2.3M views.

**And the house rule is close to the house style of the niche, not an eccentricity.** Measured
26 Aug 2026 on 627 whole transcripts across 13 channels: **77% of videos carry one outbound ask or
none**, half carry none at all, and the median last outbound ask lands at **92% of runtime**. This
corrects what `SKILL.md` used to say, that 19 of 20 competitors stack two or three asks at the
close: the real number is 23%. It also corrects the mid-roll advice on in-platform asks. The
subscribe ask in this corpus is an outro, at a median 98% of runtime, with 7% of them inside the
first fifth. Nobody here runs the one-minute subscribe, so treat that placement as borrowed from
general retention writing rather than as something the niche does.

**What the corpus cannot settle, and does not.** It measures reach, and reach is decided before
anybody hears an ask. Winners in it carry slightly *more* outbound asks than their own channel's
controls, and "fewer asks" holds on 1 channel of 13. That is not evidence against the one-ask rule;
it is evidence that this instrument cannot see the thing the rule protects, which is what the viewer
remembers the video as. The three-run control and Saraev are still the whole evidence for the rule
itself.
