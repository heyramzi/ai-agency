# Proof frames, and the three surfaces

Two small craft rules for a finished hook: how to show proof, and how to keep the visual, the
title text and the spoken line from disagreeing.

## Proof frames

`SKILL.md` carries the rule: where a real artefact exists, the proof frame is that artefact,
within two seconds of the claim. This is the detail underneath it.

**The strongest proof frame is two artefacts from one input.** One prompt, two configurations, both
outputs on screen, then a verdict that says which half each one won. A single screenshot asks the
viewer to trust the comparison; a pair hands them the control and lets them see it. The verdict is
the payload, so it has to split rather than crown: "this one won the format, that one won the
structure, so keep the first for taste and loosen the second for procedure" carries a rule the
viewer can apply, where "the second one is better" carries a preference.

It costs one real experiment and it is the cheapest authority available on a topic where everyone
else is asserting. The truth rule binds it hardest: both artefacts have to exist, come from the same
input, and be shown rather than described. One run is one run, so say so on camera.

Single source, no control test: Nate Herk, "I Deleted All My Claude Skills... And Claude Got
Smarter", 12 Aug 2026, 71k views in 5 days. 716 seconds, 2,902 words (243 wpm), 4 clips borrowed
from a 35-minute Y Combinator interview, each followed by operator translation. The A/B on two
resource guides is the only original evidence in the piece and it is what the video is remembered
for.

## Three surfaces, and the failure is that they disagree

A hook is written three times: the visual in the first frame, the title text on screen, and
the spoken line. `shorts-script`, `shorts-structures.md` holds the read-order evidence and says the spoken one
ranks last. What that reference does not say is the law that governs all three together.

**They have to carry the same claim.** A viewer who sees one thing, reads a second and hears a
third assembles nothing, and confusion is what makes people leave. It is not boredom, and on a
retention chart the two are indistinguishable. Where the surfaces agree, each one confirms the
guess the viewer made from the one before it, and that confirmation is what buys the next
sentence.

The captions and the title text are different objects and only one of them is optional. Captions
are the words being said. Title text is a larger line that is **not** spoken, carrying either the
context ("what makes a good hook") or the gap ("three ways to fix yours"). Dropping the title text
is allowed and it costs a surface, which means the spoken line and the frame then have to do all
the work alone.

### Contrast is stated directly, not implied

Both of these open a gap:

- Implied: a contrarian claim on its own. The viewer has to reconstruct what they were supposed
  to believe before they can feel the gap, and most will not bother.
- Direct: name the common belief, then break it. "Most people think the hook decides how many
  people arrive. It decides whether they stay."

The direct form travels twice the distance, because it moves the viewer to the common belief
first and only then flips them. This is the mechanism under the shock/contradiction row in
`mechanisms.md`, and it is the one that is most often half-executed: the flip is written and the
belief it flips is left in the writer's head.

The truth rule binds the belief as hard as the flip. State a belief people actually hold, not a
strawman built to be knocked down.
