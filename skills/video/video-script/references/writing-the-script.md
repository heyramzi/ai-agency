# Writing the script: bullets, beats, structure and the teach block

The craft half of this skill. `SKILL.md` holds what the measurements decided; this file holds how a script is actually written once those decisions are made.

## Every script ships as bullets

**A script is bullet points, never prose paragraphs.** The speaker reads from bullets on
camera and it comes out natural; prose makes them recite, and the recitation is audible
in the take.
 Stated 19 Aug 2026 while M0 L1's open was being rewritten, after a
paragraph-shaped draft.

**A bullet is a prompt he speaks around, not a sentence he reads out.** Under 14 words,
and the words are the thing itself: the click, the field name on screen, the number, the
point being made. Writing bullets was never the hard part; keeping them short is. On
21 Aug 2026 a generated script cleared every other rule on this page and was still
unrecordable, because every bullet was a 30 to 50 word sentence with a dash in front of
it. His words: "I'm literally not understanding what I need to record."

| | A bullet | A sentence with a dash |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | `- Settings, App Center, MCP Servers. The panel is new.` | `- This one does not get finished on camera, and the reason is the point rather than a skipped step.` |

- One bullet per beat, in the order the beat is spoken. Two ideas joined by "and" or
  "because" are two bullets.
- Nest sub-bullets for the parts of a beat, the on-screen note, or an open question.
- **No stage directions.** A bullet is a thing that gets said or done on camera, never
  an instruction about how to say it. "Land it:", "Say it plainly:", "Name the artefact:",
  "The turn:" and every cue of that family are cut. Rejected on sight 19 Aug 2026: the
  cues read as a director talking to the talent, and the talent is the author.
- **Three things are written out in full and wrapped in quotes**, and they are the only
  lines allowed past the word cap: the opening lines of the cold open, where the wording
  is load-bearing and a paraphrase loses the hook; the closing ask; and any measured
  number, which is spoken exactly and never rounded. The quotes are the marker, so no cue
  word is needed and the teleprompter styles them from the quotes alone. Everything
  between the intro and the outro is a prompt, with no exceptions but the numbers.
- Editorial notes ("On screen", "Why it is built this way", cut instructions) stay prose
  and sit outside the bullet list, because nobody says them out loud.

## The beat budget

**The body is budgeted in beats, and a beat is one bullet.** A word budget cannot be
spent any way except by lengthening bullets, which is exactly how the 21 Aug 2026 script
turned into prose: the generator's expansion pass was told to hit a word count, and it
hit it by growing the bullets it already had rather than finding the steps it had
skipped. Given a beat target the only way to hit it is to add beats.

**One beat runs about seven seconds on camera.** Measured on the 21 Aug 2026 script,
which was sized to 195 wpm before anybody looked at its bullets: 3,543 words over a
planned 20:44, carried by 179 bullets, which is 6.95 seconds a bullet. The how-to
rewrite of the same video lands in the same place at 169 beats. So the winning nineteen
minutes is roughly **158 to 185 beats**, which is the same evidence as the 3,600 to
4,200 words, converted.

- Short of the target means the block is **missing beats**, never missing words. Find the
  step it skipped: the exact click, the field name on screen, the number the viewer sees,
  the mistake most people make here and what it costs.
- The word count is still recorded, because it is what the three-run control measured. It
  is a reading of the script, not a target to hit.
- The teleprompter paces in **beats per minute**, not words per minute, for the same
  reason: the body is spoken around rather than read, so its word count says how much was
  typed and not how long the take runs.


## Structure

Eight blocks. The beat budget is the constraint that keeps them honest.

| Block | Share | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cold open | 0:00-0:30 | Owned by `video-hooks`. Take it from there. |
| The failure | ~8% | Your own, told in full. The buyer's life in your words. |
| The stakes | ~7% | What it costs to keep doing it the current way. One real number, with its artefact. |
| The mechanism | ~15% | The thing that makes it work, named. Lead with this, never with the promise. |
| Build 1..n | ~50% | The teach. One idea per block, each ending in a state the viewer can see on screen. |
| The proof | ~12% | The artefact doing the thing. Two configurations from one input beats one screenshot. |
| The honest limit | ~5% | Who this does not work for, said plainly. It is what makes the rest credible. |
| The ask | last 20s | One outbound ask. Named product, named price, one link. |

**Every build block ends on a visible state change.** A block that ends on a
sentence is a paragraph; a block that ends on the board looking different is a
beat. This is what carries the open loop the completion data rewards.

## The teach block, which is 53% of the script

Everything above governs the shape of a script. This governs what happens inside a
build block, which is where half the runtime is spent and where the skill was silent
until 23 Aug 2026.

Read off a full transcription of Nick Saraev's `CLAUDE CODE FULL COURSE 4 HOURS`
(`QoQBzR1NIqI`, 4:10:42, 62,139 words, 35 chapters, 2,314,591 views on 502k subs, a
4.6x channel multiplier, read 23 Aug 2026). **Single source, no control test.** It
sits below anything measured on this channel, and it is here because the alternative
was nothing.

**One portable idea, restated at the close of every build.** His four hours run on a
single loop, named once at 00:39:35: give it the task, let it do the task, make it
verify the result. Every module afterwards is an instance of it, and he says the
absence of that loop is why people fail. A script carrying one idea restated six
times holds longer than one carrying six ideas listed once. Name the idea in the
mechanism block, then point at it again as each build lands.

**Say the block's line at the start of the block as well as at its close.** The rule above puts the
restatement at the end of a build. Put the same sentence at the start of it too, so the block
opens on a claim and every excursion has somewhere to return to instead of a summary it
eventually reaches. At video scale this is the hook and belongs to `video-hooks`; the addition
here is block scale, one line per build. The return sits on the beat where the build lands, never
on the last beat, because the last beat is the seam and it belongs to the next question.

**The line carries a third to a half of the idea, never all of it.** A sentence written to
hold the whole block becomes a slogan, and a slogan is the thing a speaker cannot say out
loud and so never says. "Leadership is certainty" is enough to start on, and the rest is fed
in as the block develops. **It comes back in nearly the same words**, because what a viewer
repeats afterwards is a phrase and not a paragraph: one coached client closed a company
speech on "we go where the money flows" five times unchanged, and that is the sentence the
room left with. Single source and unmeasured, 31 Aug 2026, Joseph Tsar, `How To Never Ramble
When You Speak`, a communication coach rather than a channel, so it sits under everything the
three-run control decided. It is also the writing half of the recording habit in
`video-coach`, which is what a take with nothing to return to costs in restarts.

**Draw the abstraction.** Any idea that is not already a thing on screen gets drawn
as one, on the same screen, in the middle of the walkthrough. A ship crossing the
Atlantic with a stiff rudder, for why the instruction file steers everything. A
staircase from 80% to 100%, for why speed beats one-shot accuracy. A primacy curve,
for why the important rule goes at the top. This is the highest-yield device in the
reference video and it costs nothing but the seconds. Drawing on the shared screen is
not b-roll, so it survives the doctrine's fifth decision. The `whiteboard` skill builds
them.

**Define the term the moment it appears, in objects the viewer already owns.** "An
IDE is a file explorer plus a notepad plus ChatGPT, in one." "A token is like a word,
just a few more." "A dot in front of a folder hides it." Four seconds each, and it is
what lets a technical build stay legible to a non-technical buyer. A term used before
it is defined loses the viewer silently: they do not comment, they leave.

**Show it failing, once per build block, and do not cut it.** Not one honest moment
per video. Broken buttons, cropped faces, an error message read out, 987 of 989
records processed and the number said. He fixes each one on camera. This is the trust
engine of a walkthrough and it is the thing a demo cannot fake. It also pairs with the
existing rule: **a block that ends on the thing working, having shown it not working,
is a visible state change twice over.**

**When the block is a verdict on a tool, make the tool produce the number.** Do not
argue that the thing is bad, ask it to audit itself on camera and read the answer out.
Theo's 39-minute memory video is the clean version: he asks Claude Code what it has
stored, then how much of it earns its keep, then how often it is read, and the tool
returns 3 writes for every read, 26 of 45 files never opened once. He arrives skeptical
and leaves gutting the feature across his fleet, and the escalation is real because he
did not know the number before he asked. Two conditions make it work: the question has
to be answerable by the tool from its own data, and you have to ask it before you have
decided. A demo that confirms a verdict you already announced is a re-enactment, and it
reads as one.

**The build is real work out of the business, never a toy.** His four hours build his
own agency site, a PandaDoc replacement with signing and payment, a lead scraper, and
a classifier run over 989 real emails at 0.36 seconds each. Nothing is a to-do app,
and the counts are said out loud. A demo built for the camera teaches the tool; a
build the founder was going to do anyway teaches the job, and it is the only version
that can carry the doctrine's sixth decision, the guide that contains the product.

**Cost in money and minutes, never a percentage.** $17 a month against the month's
output. 35 minutes without a plan against 10 with. A percentage is an argument; a
number of minutes is a thing the viewer can picture losing. The truth rule binds
hardest here, so every figure is measured and named to its artefact.

**Answer the dating problem out loud, in the mechanism block, in one sentence.** Any
video about a tool that ships weekly decays from the day it publishes. He spends one
sentence at 00:04:36: the screens will look different by the time you watch this, what
matters is knowing where to find the current version, and here is where that is. It
costs nothing, it widens the shelf life, and it is the same trust move as refusing the
category's promise in `video-hooks`.

**Dictate the prompts on camera, and say why once.** Every substantial prompt in the
reference video is spoken, with the arithmetic stated a single time: about 60 words a
minute typed against about 200 spoken. For a viewer who does not code, this is what
turns prompting from writing into talking, and it removes the largest unspoken
objection in the room. Production note rather than a script rule, but the script is
where it gets planned.

**A guide is navigated, not watched.** 35 chapters across four hours. Ship one chapter
per block, named for the state the viewer reaches rather than the feature being shown:
"the Project that stops the re-explaining", never "Projects".

## When the body is an argument, not a build

The eight blocks and the teach-block rules above assume a build: a screen, a click, a state
change. A thesis video has none of that. Its body is a claim about the world, and its failure
mode is not a thin script, it is a chronology.

Everything in this section is read off one reference: theyo, `cZtvi1YzNII`, "Pourquoi les SaaS
sont en train de disparaître ?", 22:38, 4,660 words, 206 wpm, published 27 Aug 2026. **It carries
no performance evidence.** 8,023 views on 110k subscribers a day after publish, no channel median,
no control test. It is here for its moves, and an unmeasured move is a candidate rather than a
rule. Nothing below overrides anything the three-run control decided. It also publishes no
chapters at all, against the 78% norm and against the checklist in `SKILL.md`; with no results
behind it that is a data point, not a licence.

**The chain is what stops a history from being a list.** The reference travels 1997 Clippy, 2000
Salesforce, 25 years of stacking, Nov 2022 ChatGPT, 2023 plugins, late 2024 MCP, 2026 now. Seven
dates, and it is not a timeline, because **every link ends on what was still missing, and that
lack is the reason the next link exists.** Clippy watched the typing and knew nothing about the
intent. Salesforce moved where software lived and left the user carrying intent between tools.
ChatGPT understood the request and could not act on it. Plugins could act, on models too weak to.
MCP connected everything and settled nothing about permission. Write each link by naming the lack
first and the date second: the date is the peg, the lack is the beat. This is `storytelling`'s open
loop run five times in series, and it is the only thing that makes the same material read as an
argument instead of as background.

**Open on a dated object, and make it come back as the answer.** The video opens on a paperclip
put into Word in 1997, not on its thesis. The thesis question arrives forty seconds later and is
parked out loud: to understand this, we go back. The paperclip then returns twice, changed. Once
as the thing that could never have fixed the problem. Once as the thing ChatGPT finally was, "the
promise of Clippy 25 years later". That is `storytelling`'s motif rule with the constraint
tightened: **in an argument video the motif is not decoration, it is the answer, and an object
that cannot come back as the answer is the wrong object.** Pick the one the thesis is secretly
about.

**Open on what the room has already dismissed.** Clippy is a joke the viewer settled twenty years
ago, which is what makes the shock score low and the proof fast: the claim is that the joke was the
prototype, and a single sentence moves them. A thesis that rehabilitates something the room already
mocks starts with attention rather than asking for it.

**Find the scene somebody actually staged.** "SaaS changed how software was delivered" is a
definition. Salesforce throwing a launch party themed The End of Software, then paying actors to
picket a competitor's conference with anti-software placards while being a software company, is a
scene, and it carries the same claim with the irony attached. For any abstract turning point the
research job is to find the thing a person physically did. It is `storytelling`'s specific-beats-
abstract, and in a video with no build blocks it does the work the demo would.

**Caveat the number in place, in one sentence, and keep using it.** Airtable at $11bn in 2021
against a $1.3bn acquisition in Aug 2026, still carrying $480m of ARR. The next sentence says AI
does not explain that on its own, they sold the tool and kept the AI technology. The number
survives, and it is now a number he can be trusted with. The honest-limit block is this move at
block scale, at ~5% of runtime; this is the same move at sentence scale, spent on the one figure
strong enough to be doubted, where it lands rather than eight minutes later.

**Ask the title question twice, and refuse the cheap answer both times.** At the midpoint: are SaaS
disappearing, the easy answer is a big yes, in reality it is more complicated than that. At the
close: are SaaS going to disappear, no, not at all, this is an evolution of the format. A thesis
video that answers its own headline in the negative keeps the headline honest and pays the loop
with a real answer instead of the one the title implied. The midpoint refusal is what buys the
second half.

**Fence the prediction out loud, once.** "Now I am going to play fortune teller, here are my takes,
we will see whether the future says I was right." Then three, numbered. This is how a script held
to the truth rule gets to say unmeasurable things: **the register switch is the permission, and
without it a prediction reads as a claim.** One sentence, and it protects every measured number
in the rest of the script.

**Close by giving permission, then ask.** Twenty-two minutes arguing the category is being eaten,
and the last block says the model is still the best business there is, there are still mammoths to
hunt, and if you sit waiting for the next Claude release you will never build anything. Then the
one outbound ask, in the final fifteen seconds, which is the ask rule intact. The ask lands on
somebody who has just been released rather than somebody who has just been frightened. An argument
video that ends on its own bad news converts worse than the same video ending on what is now
allowed, and the ask does not move to fix it.

## Camera language on the outside, mechanics on the inside

Your own marketing doctrine sets this and it binds every
script that sells. On camera the words are the ones a founder already uses:
"batches, sprints of one week", "a clear view on your team's capacity without
hiring a project manager", "billable time". The named mechanics, Points, the
slipped tag, the space model, are explained **inside** the install, never in the
part that sells it.

A build walkthrough sits on both sides of that line, so the split is by block
rather than by video: the teach blocks may name a mechanic once they are showing
it on screen, and the failure, the stakes and the ask stay in camera language
throughout. A viewer who has to learn a vocabulary before they understand the
stakes has already left.

## Register

Short declaratives. The real noun instead of the abstraction. Concrete numbers with
the artefact that proves them, never a rounded estimate standing in for a
measurement. Contrast pairs carry the teaching: what most people do, then what
actually works, then why the difference is mechanical rather than a matter of
taste.

The one rule that overrides register, carried from `video-hooks`: **go as hard as
you like on the mechanism, and every fact underneath it has to be true.** An account
that sells on being the founder who shows the real numbers loses that on one invented
figure, and on nothing else.
