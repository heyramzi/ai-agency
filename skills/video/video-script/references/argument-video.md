# When the body is an argument, not a build

The eight blocks and the teach-block rules in [`writing-the-script.md`](writing-the-script.md)
assume a build: a screen, a click, a state change. A thesis video has none of that. Its body is a
claim about the world, and its failure mode is not a thin script, it is a chronology.

Everything here is read off one reference: theyo, `cZtvi1YzNII`, "Pourquoi les SaaS sont en train
de disparaître ?", 22:38, 4,660 words, 206 wpm, published 27 Aug 2026. **It carries no performance
evidence**: 8,023 views on 110k subscribers a day after publish, no channel median, no control
test. It is here for its moves, and an unmeasured move is a candidate rather than a rule. Nothing
below overrides anything the three-run control decided. It also publishes no chapters at all,
against the 78% norm and the checklist in `SKILL.md`; with no results behind it that is a data
point, not a licence.

**The chain is what stops a history from being a list.** The reference travels 1997 Clippy, 2000
Salesforce, 25 years of stacking, Nov 2022 ChatGPT, 2023 plugins, late 2024 MCP, 2026 now. Seven
dates, and it is not a timeline, because **every link ends on what was still missing, and that
lack is the reason the next link exists.** Clippy watched the typing and knew nothing about the
intent. Salesforce moved where software lived and left the user carrying intent between tools.
ChatGPT understood the request and could not act on it. Plugins could act, on models too weak to.
MCP connected everything and settled nothing about permission. Write each link by naming the lack
first and the date second: the date is the peg, the lack is the beat. This is `storytelling`'s
open loop run five times in series, the only thing that makes the same material read as an
argument instead of background.

**Open on a dated object, and make it come back as the answer.** The video opens on a paperclip
put into Word in 1997, not on its thesis. The thesis question arrives forty seconds later and is
parked out loud: to understand this, we go back. The paperclip then returns twice, changed: once
as the thing that could never have fixed the problem, once as the thing ChatGPT finally was, "the
promise of Clippy 25 years later". That is `storytelling`'s motif rule with the constraint
tightened: **in an argument video the motif is not decoration, it is the answer, and an object
that cannot come back as the answer is the wrong object.** Pick the one the thesis is secretly
about.

**Open on what the room has already dismissed.** Clippy is a joke the viewer settled twenty years
ago, which is what makes the shock score low and the proof fast: the claim is that the joke was
the prototype, and a single sentence moves them. A thesis that rehabilitates something the room
already mocks starts with attention rather than asking for it.

**Find the scene somebody actually staged.** "SaaS changed how software was delivered" is a
definition. Salesforce throwing a launch party themed The End of Software, then paying actors to
picket a competitor's conference with anti-software placards while being a software company, is a
scene, and it carries the same claim with the irony attached. For any abstract turning point the
research job is to find the thing a person physically did: `storytelling`'s specific-beats-
abstract, doing in a build-free video the work the demo would.

**Caveat the number in place, in one sentence, and keep using it.** Airtable at $11bn in 2021
against a $1.3bn acquisition in Aug 2026, still carrying $480m of ARR. The next sentence says AI
does not explain that on its own, they sold the tool and kept the AI technology. The number
survives, and it is now a number he can be trusted with. The honest-limit block is this move at
block scale, at ~5% of runtime; this is the same move at sentence scale, spent on the one figure
strong enough to be doubted, where it lands rather than eight minutes later.

**Ask the title question twice, and refuse the cheap answer both times.** At the midpoint: are
SaaS disappearing, the easy answer is a big yes, in reality it is more complicated than that. At
the close: are SaaS going to disappear, no, not at all, this is an evolution of the format. A
thesis video that answers its own headline in the negative keeps the headline honest and pays the
loop with a real answer instead of the one the title implied. The midpoint refusal is what buys
the second half.

**Fence the prediction out loud, once.** "Now I am going to play fortune teller, here are my
takes, we will see whether the future says I was right." Then three, numbered. This is how a
script held to the truth rule gets to say unmeasurable things: **the register switch is the
permission, and without it a prediction reads as a claim.** One sentence, and it protects every
measured number in the rest of the script.

**Close by giving permission, then ask.** Twenty-two minutes arguing the category is being eaten,
and the last block says the model is still the best business there is, there are still mammoths to
hunt, and if you sit waiting for the next Claude release you will never build anything. Then the
one outbound ask, in the final fifteen seconds, the ask rule intact. The ask lands on somebody who
has just been released rather than somebody who has just been frightened. An argument video that
ends on its own bad news converts worse than the same video ending on what is now allowed, and the
ask does not move to fix it.
