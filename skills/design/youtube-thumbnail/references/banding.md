# Band the niche before you design anything

Step 2 of the workflow, and the only expensive one. Everything after it is cheap
because of it.

## The problem banding solves

Take the fifteen best-performing thumbnails on a channel you admire and list what they
have in common. You will get twenty traits: a face, a bold sans, a yellow accent, a
three-quarter crop, a plain background. Every one of those is also true of that
channel's fifteen worst thumbnails.

You have measured the channel's house style, not its levers, and house style is the
part you cannot use anyway.

**Only a trait that separates the two bands is a lever.**

## Building the bands

For each channel you want to learn from:

1. **Pull its catalogue.** Title, publish date, duration, view count, format. Any
   YouTube data client will do this; so will a scrape of the uploads playlist.
2. **Split long-form from Shorts and score them separately.** Their feeds behave
   nothing alike and mixing them destroys the signal.
3. **Compute that channel's own median views for the format**, over the same period.
   Never use raw views and never compare across channels: a 40,000-view video is a
   flop on one channel and a breakout on another.
4. **Winner band**: the frames well above that channel's median. **Control band**: the
   frames well below it. Aim for roughly 15 winners and 12 controls per channel.
   Leave the middle out; it tells you nothing and it dilutes both ends.
5. **Build two contact sheets per channel** and put them side by side. The comparison
   has to be visual and simultaneous. Scrolling between two folders does not work.

Age is a confound: a video published last week has not finished accumulating views. Cut
anything younger than about 30 days, or normalise by age.

## Reading them

Go trait by trait, not frame by frame. For each candidate trait, count how many winners
carry it and how many controls carry it, then write both numbers down.

Three outcomes:

- **Present in both bands** -> house style. Note it and move on. Copying it buys nothing.
- **Concentrated in the winners** -> candidate lever.
- **Concentrated in the controls** -> candidate anti-pattern, and these are the more
  useful of the two, because they are the things you are probably already doing.

Traits worth counting, as a starting list: number of distinct elements; a person present;
person size as a fraction of frame height; face to camera or not; a second person; text
word count; text makes a claim or names a category; a number in the text; a screenshot
present and whether it is readable; count of logos; number of saturated colours; an
arrow; a crossed-out option.

## The replication bar

**One channel is an anecdote.** A trait that separates the bands on a single channel is
that channel's audience, not your niche. Write nothing into a rulebook until it runs the
same direction on five or more independent channels, or until you are explicitly labelling
it as a single-channel lever.

Ten channels at 15 and 12 gives 270 frames, which is enough to see a five-channel pattern
and not enough to be statistical. Say so when you write it up. A count from one reader in
one sitting is directional, and the classification of a borderline frame is a judgement
call. Publish the counts so the judgement can be argued with.

## Include your own channel as the tenth

Band your own frames exactly the same way and read them last. The point is not to find
what wins for you, which is a small sample, but to find the faults that show up on
**both** of your bands. Those are your house style, and they are the ones costing you
something on every upload without ever appearing as a bad result.

## Write it up as a file, not as a conversation

One document per niche, holding: the per-channel table (median, what separates its
winners), the findings that replicated with their counts, and the method notes so the
whole thing can be re-run and disagreed with.

That file, not this skill, becomes the source of truth for every frame you design
afterwards. Re-run it when the catalogues have drifted, roughly once or twice a year.
