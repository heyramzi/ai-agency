# The words on the frame

Step 5 of the workflow, and the step most often treated as a caption for a picture that
was already decided.

The cleanest evidence for this comes from any channel that shoots the same composition
over and over. When the picture is constant across both bands, the picture cannot be the
lever, and what is left is the label. Look for such a channel in your own banding pass:
it is the fastest proof you will find that copy carries most of the click.

The pattern that shows up there, again and again: **every winner carries a number or a
clock; every control names a category or states a sentiment.**

## The law

**Two to four words. Make a claim. Bound it. Say what the title does not.**

Five words is the ceiling and only when the five are a sentence with a full stop. The
title carries the specifics; the frame carries the one thing that makes the specifics
worth a click.

## Six formulas

Pick one. Do not blend two.

### 1. The bounded scope

Names the whole of the thing, so the viewer knows nothing is missing.

> `full guide` · `FULL COURSE 4 HOURS` · `2026 FULL TOUR` · `LEARN NO-CODE IN 3 HOURS`

Works when the video really is the complete version. Burns trust fast if it is not.

### 2. The exact cost

Names what it costs the viewer in time or effort, low enough to be worth trying.

> `10 minutes/day` · `2 MINUTES A DAY` · `LEARN IN 30 MINUTES` · `ONLY 1 PROMPT` · `NO SETUP!`

Usually the strongest single family in a set.

### 3. The count

A small round number of things, where the number is the surprise.

> `A CEO ONLY HAS 3 JOBS` · `BUILD THESE 3 SYSTEMS` · `in just 7 steps`

Small counts only. Three, five, seven. Pairs well with formula 2 rather than competing.

### 4. The deadline

A wall-clock time, which is more concrete than a duration.

> `DO IT ALL BY 10AM` · `finish everything by 10:00am`

Rare but clean, and the only formula that puts the viewer's own day in the frame.

### 5. The negation

A row of crossed-out options and one survivor. The frame does the arguing.

> `THEY ALL LOST` · `IT'S A TRAP` · `Stop Paying For APIs` · `OLD -> NEW`

Needs the picture to carry the crossed-out set, so it is a copy-and-composition pair,
not a label.

### 6. The statement with a full stop

A short declarative that sounds like a verdict. The full stop is doing work.

> `YOUR IPAD. BUT BETTER.` · `GET THE IPAD RIGHT.` · `Desk Candy.`

Faceless variant, in practice.

## The ban list

**An adjective with no object.** `INSANE AGENTS`, `UNLIMITED CONTENT`, `GAMECHANGER`,
`Thank Me Later`. If you can delete the adjective and lose nothing, the adjective was
the whole idea.

**A category name.** `CRM DASHBOARD`, `AI Agent`, `DOCS`. A category tells the viewer
which shelf it is on, not why to take it down.

**Series numbering as the promise.** `MINI COURSE DAY 1`. Day 1 of what, and why today.

**A restatement of the title.** Two competing sentences reading the same idea. The frame
should be the sentence the title could not fit.

**A scold.** `DON'T MAKE THIS MISTAKE`. The negation formula works because something
survives the crossing-out; a scold leaves the viewer with nothing to take.

**Somebody else's number.** `ADDED $60,000 IN 8 MONTHS`. Your own zero-to-X ladder is
the one exception.

**Any word past the fourth**, unless the whole thing is one short sentence.

## Case, colour and plate

Three treatments that hold up, and the choice is a personality decision rather than a rule:

- **Lowercase white, no plate**, over a photograph with real depth. Reads as a caption on
  a film still. Needs a dark or busy-but-dim background.
- **Heavy caps on a solid colour tab.** The most reliable of the three; the tab is what
  makes it survive 320px.
- **Black on white, or white on black, no colour.** Works when the photograph is already
  the whole idea and the label is only naming it.

Whichever: one weight, one size, one block. A second text block at a second size is the
most common way a frame ends up at eight elements.

## Writing procedure

1. Write the title as published. Underline the specific promise inside it.
2. Write the one sentence from step 3: what the eye lands on, and what it tells the viewer.
3. Generate one candidate per formula. Six candidates, two to four words each.
4. Delete every candidate that restates the title, names a category, or carries an
   adjective you could remove.
5. Read the survivors at arm's length from a phone. Keep the two that still make a claim.
6. The best goes on the frame. The second becomes variant B, words changed and nothing else.

## A note on withholding

A count with the items hidden is stronger than a count with the items listed. `5 spaces.
that's it.` beside five name plates blurred to illegibility asks a question the title
cannot answer, and the only way to get the answer is to click. Naming all five closes the
loop for free.

This works because it is honest: the five exist, they are on screen, they are simply not
readable yet. A blur over nothing is a trick and it costs the next click.
