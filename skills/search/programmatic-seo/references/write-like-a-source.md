# Writing a page a machine can lift from

Read this after the draft exists and before the pre-publish gate. It is the craft half of Phase 5:
[geo-signals.md](geo-signals.md) holds the evidence for why each rule is here and
[seo-checklist.md](seo-checklist.md) turns them into pass-or-fail lines.

The job is narrow. A retrieval system cuts your page into chunks, and you do not control where the
cuts fall. So every chunk has to survive alone: still true, still specific, and still naming you.
A page that reads well top to bottom and dissolves into nothing when a single section is lifted out
is the common failure, and it fails silently.

## The four rules the chunking imposes

- **Answer first.** Open every section with the conclusion, then the support. Both people and
  models weight the start and end of a passage over the middle, so a point buried three paragraphs
  in is missed by both. A question-shaped H2 answered in the first sentence under it is the whole
  technique.
- **Atomic sections.** Take any H2 and read it with nothing around it. If it needs three earlier
  paragraphs to make sense, rewrite it.
- **Name the entities.** "This tool finds low-difficulty keywords" carries nothing. "Ahrefs
  Keywords Explorer finds keywords with low difficulty and high traffic potential" carries a
  product, a capability and two attributes. A lifted chunk full of "this platform" and "the
  solution" cannot credit anyone, so it does not.
- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence needs two reads, it is too long for the chunker and for
  the reader. Subject, verb, object.

## Marketing language is invisible, and here is what that looks like

The rewrite below is a real audit of a Miami plumbing site. Every left-hand string appears on
hundreds of competitor pages, which is exactly why no model has a reason to quote this one.

| On the page | Rewritten | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "We take pride in providing reliable and expert plumbing services to homeowners throughout Miami" | "Licensed Miami plumbers, same-day service, no call-out fee" | The original carries zero verifiable claims. The rewrite carries three |
| "With years of experience and a dedicated team of skilled professionals" | "Resolves plumbing issues of all sizes, from leaky faucets to sewer line replacement" | "Years of experience" and "dedicated team" appear on every site in the category, so they carry no information and cannot distinguish anyone |
| H1: "Expert Plumber in Miami, Reliable Local Plumbing Services" | H1: "Licensed Plumber in Miami" | "Expert" is a self-declared adjective a model assigns no weight. "Licensed" is a fact that can be checked |
| H2: "Miami's Premier Plumbing Services" | H2: "Plumbing services in Miami" | Nobody has ever searched for premier plumbing services |
| "Detecting and fixing leaks to avoid water damage and high utility bills" | "Leak detection and repair that stops water damage before it spreads" | The original says two things at once and neither clearly. A single tight statement is what gets quoted whole |

Two failures on that page are worth naming separately because they cost more than any single
sentence:

**A list of twenty vague bullets loses the whole block, not one line.** Items like "friendly
customer support" and "professional workmanship" carry no citable fact, and a list where no item is
specific reads as filler, so the retrieval skips the block entirely. Lists earn their weight only
when each item stands alone as a fact.

**An unsupported claim is worse than no claim.** The page said "fully licensed and insured" with no
licence number, no insurer, no document, nowhere on the site. A model has nothing to corroborate
and no reason to repeat it. Every claim of standing carries its evidence in the same breath, or it
is cut.

## The test before publishing

Ask it as one sentence: **could another site in this category have published this exact page?** If
yes, it is not additive and the summariser will replace it with one paragraph of consensus. What
survives that test is what a model cannot generate: what you measured, what a client actually said,
what failed, the screenshot, the number, the opinion you will defend.

The strongest published shape for this is a topic people are already discussing, plus your own
data on it, plus your read of what the data means. That combination is not summarisable, which is
why it gets cited rather than absorbed.

## Cite other people, so other people cite you

The largest single lift on the Princeton table is adding citations to your own sources, at +40%.
Link two or three authoritative sources per page behind specific claims: government and academic
domains, industry reports, named studies. The effect is not politeness. A page whose claims trace
to somewhere is a page a model can ground an answer on, and grounding is what it is selecting for.

Statistics and named, titled quotations follow at +37% and +30%. Specific numbers near the claim
they support, not collected in a box at the bottom.

## Label your frameworks with your brand name

Models flatten originality: an idea only you talk about is absorbed as general knowledge and
credited to nobody. "The <brand> content scoring matrix", defined explicitly and repeated across
the blog, the social posts, the podcast and the video, survives that. "A content scoring matrix"
does not.

## The FAQ is a placement decision

Put it high on the page, not below the testimonials and the service-area block. Retrieval budgets
are finite and the bottom of a long page is where a section goes to be skipped.

Source it from the call logs, the DMs and the support tickets rather than inventing it. Every
question a customer has actually asked is a long-tail query somebody is typing into an assistant in
full sentences, and answering it plainly on the page is how a small business wins queries no large
competitor has the patience to cover. Nobody reads forty FAQ entries and a model reads all of them.

## Reading level and length

Write at roughly an eighth to tenth grade level. That is clarity, not simplification: the
technical accuracy stays and the sentence construction gets out of its way.

**Length is whatever the answer needs.** Word count does not correlate with citation and over half
of cited pages run under 1,000 words. Padding to a target adds chunks that say nothing, and a
controlled test found that a substantial block of added explanatory copy, positive for LLM traffic,
was measurably negative for Google rankings on the same pages. Depth of coverage across the topic
is the goal, not length on one page.

## Freshness is a schedule, not an intention

Diarise a real refresh. Three independent readings put the decay window between 30 and 90 days, and
a page untouched for a year and a half loses citations it used to win even with its backlinks
intact. A touched date is detected and does nothing. Update the numbers, the examples and the
screenshots, and say what changed.

The cheapest refresh target is a page that used to rank and has slipped: it already holds the
authority, so the content is the only broken part. Check first that it had referring domains to
begin with, because a page that never had links has a different problem and rewriting it will not
fix that.

## Surface what is already written but buried

Content locked inside a PDF, a slide deck, an ebook gate or a social feed does nothing for the
brand's authority, because the crawler either cannot reach it or does not attribute it to the site.
One medical manufacturer lost about half its organic traffic **and conversions** to AI Overviews,
and recovered with a 30% gain by moving the substance out of PDFs and onto ordinary pages. No new
writing was involved.

The same applies to a large social following: the audience is an asset and the posts sitting on
someone else's platform are not an authority signal for your domain. Republish the substance on the
site as a resource library or a set of pages.
